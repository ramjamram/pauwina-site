Chapter 2

Your life is somewhere on the ocean

For the next six to ten weeks there's nothing to lift. But there's one piece of paper you need to understand, and a few emails to watch for.

The bill of lading is your ticket

The bill of lading (B/L) is the receipt and ownership document for your container. Whoever holds it can claim the goods. It comes in two kinds:

Original (paper)

It has to be couriered to you, signed on the back, and handed to the shipping line before release. If it's late, your container waits, and storage charges pile up.

Telex / express release

Released electronically. No courier and no lost paper. You usually sign a short Letter of Undertaking instead. Ask for this.

If a forwarder booked your shipment, there may be two bills of lading: theirs (a "house" B/L) and the shipping line's. In Manila you'll deal with the one where you're named as consignee.

Track it yourself

Every container has a number: four letters and seven digits, like MSBU2735321. Type it into the shipping line's "track a shipment" page. ETAs slip often, so check every few days.

The arrival notice

About a week before the ship docks, the shipping line emails an arrival notice. It's the most useful page you'll get. Look for:

  • Estimated date of arrival: your clocks start from the actual unloading.
  • Port of discharge vs. terminal of pick-up: they can be different places, such as Manila North Harbour and MICT.
  • Consignee: it should be your name, spelled exactly as in your passport.
  • Type of document: "Original" means you need the paper B/L in hand.
  • The small print: how many free days you get, and when storage starts.
