Chapter 5

Yes, you can do customs yourself

The law doesn't require a broker. If you're the consignee, you can file. It takes patience and a folder of copies.

Need the messages to send Customs and the shipping line? See How to Clear a Container of Household Goods in Manila.

Your folder

At the customs office

  1. Ask for the section that handles personal and household effects. It's usually the informal entry desk at that port.
  2. Lodge your declaration and PHED. If you're asking for the returning-resident exemption, bring your Tax Exemption Indorsement request from the DOF Revenue Office, or ask how to file it there.
  3. X-ray, and maybe an inspection. Be there in person. If they open the container, your clear list makes it quick.
  4. Assessment. Customs sets a value and computes duty (about 15%) and VAT (12%). Ask for the computation in writing.
  5. Pay at the cashier or the bank they name. Keep every official receipt.

If the assessment looks wrong

Don't pay and walk away angry. Ask how they reached the value, show your used values and receipts, and ask about filing a protest. The deadline is short, so ask on the spot.

Do it yourself, or hire a broker?

The law doesn't require a broker if you're the consignee: you can file the declaration yourself. Here's how to decide.

Doing it yourself makes sense if…A broker is worth it if…
Your name is on the bill of lading as consigneeSomeone else has to clear it for you, or you're still abroad
You live near the port and can take several weekdays offYou can't take weekdays off, or you live far from the port
It's ordinary used household goodsThere's a vehicle, many of the same item, or anything needing a permit
Your papers are complete: bill of lading, valued list, PHED, passportsCustoms flags your shipment for a full inspection, or the assessment is disputed
You're patient with queues and repeat visitsYou're up against the free days or the 15-day deadline

What a broker is, and what only they can do

A customs broker is a licensed professional regulated by the Professional Regulation Commission (PRC) under the Customs Brokers Act (RA 9280). When you hire one, the declaration is filed and signed under their license. Before you hire, ask for their PRC license number and check it. People who aren't licensed shouldn't be preparing or filing customs paperwork for a fee, whatever they call themselves.

What brokers should charge

There is an official schedule of brokerage fees (Customs Administrative Order 1-2001). Household goods usually go through informal entry, where the official brokerage fee is ₱469.22 to ₱700 per entry. For formal entries, the fee rises with the shipment's dutiable value; above ₱200,000 it is ₱5,300 plus 0.375% of the amount over ₱200,000.

In practice brokers often charge more than the schedule, and the schedule itself is being reviewed by the Bureau of Customs and the brokers' association. But it tells you something important: the broker's own fee should be a small part of your total. Most of a big quote is usually duties and VAT, port and shipping-line charges, storage, and trucking. Those are paid to someone else, with official receipts.

Got a big "all-in" quote? Ask these five questions

  1. How much of this is duties and VAT, and how did you compute it?
  2. How much is port, terminal and shipping-line charges?
  3. How much is storage or container late fees, and from what date?
  4. How much is trucking?
  5. What is your own professional fee, and what's your PRC license number?
A good broker answers all five in writing and gives you official receipts for everything paid to the government, port and shipping line. If they won't, get another quote. Use our duty estimator to sanity-check the duties.

If you do hire one

  • Hire for the filing only if you can handle the shipping line and trucking yourself. It's cheaper.
  • Get the fee in writing before you hand over documents.
  • Keep copies of everything you give them, and ask for copies of everything they file.
  • Don't hand over cash for "facilitation" without an official receipt.
